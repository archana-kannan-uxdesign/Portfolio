WayATL

Wayfinding for the 2026 FIFA World Cup

WayATL is a location-aware progressive web app built to help FIFA World Cup fans navigate the city during one of the largest events Atlanta has ever hosted. As Lead UX Designer, I led research and design for a wayfinding experience built for a multilingual, high-volume crowd operating under real safety and operational constraints.

01 — Problem

Before the first sign

Context

For the 2026 FIFA World Cup, hundreds of thousands of international and out-of-state fans will arrive in Atlanta, most of them finding their way through an unfamiliar city in an unfamiliar language. Without clear, accessible wayfinding, the fan experience starts to break down before match day even begins.

A partner team designed the city’s physical signage: static, in English, and useful once a fan is close enough to read it. Our challenge was the gap before that point: reaching fans sooner, wherever they start, long before they ever see a sign.

Problem statement

Fans arriving in Atlanta for the World Cup are navigating a city most have never visited, often in a language other than English, with no single, reliable source for transit, nearby essentials or emergency information. Static signage and generic map apps weren’t built to close that gap at the moment a fan needs it most: standing on an unfamiliar corner, unsure where to go next.

How might we

…give a multilingual crowd of first-time visitors instant access to exactly what they need, right where they’re standing, with no app download, no login and no prior knowledge of the city?

02 — Opportunity

The answer came first

We didn’t start from a blank page. The solution was decided before research began.

WayATL grew out of SCADpro’s founding pitch: the proposal that brought the school and the Metro Atlanta Chamber on board was built around one specific concept. By the time our team joined, the format wasn’t something to discover. It was the premise the client had already signed on to.

  • Given 01

    Mobile web, not an app

    Opens in the browser. No download, no account, no login.

  • Given 02

    Launched from the street

    A QR code or NFC tap on the totems and signage already going up across the city.

  • Given 03

    Aware of where you stand

    Each touchpoint knows its location and serves what a fan needs from that exact spot.

03 — Research

Listening before mapping

Secondary research areas

  • User research and synthesis
  • User journey and persona development
  • Event facts and statistics
  • Digital accessibility and ethical urban design
  • Back-end data intelligence gathering
  • Wayfinding and mega-event case studies
  • Technology feasibility and implementation at scale

Insight 01

People get lost at handoff points, not on long walks

Fans lose their way at moments of transition: arriving at the airport, transferring on MARTA, walking to the stadium and pushing through the exit crush. Wayfinding should be concentrated where one mode of travel ends and the next begins.

Insight 02

Trust comes before translation

Fans arrive with different cultural needs, and for some, privacy is the only reason they’ll use the system at all. Staying anonymous, with no login, is a baseline requirement, not an extra.

Insight 03

Data has to benefit the neighborhoods too

Every scan adds to a picture of how people move through the city. That intelligence should support Atlanta’s historically overlooked neighborhoods, not just its commercial districts.

User personas

Four fans, four reasons to need WayATL

The First-Time International

Motivations
Experience Southern hospitality, follow their team and find the best local food.
Pain points
Language barriers, fear of getting lost in a city that isn’t built for walking, and unpredictable MARTA schedules.

The High-Stakes Pilgrim

Motivations
Express deep national pride, take part in group rituals and find social spaces.
Pain points
High sensitivity to extreme heat, a need to move as a coordinated group, and legal or cultural constraints.

The At-Risk Local Diaspora

Motivations
Witness a historic sporting moment while staying safe in a complex political climate.
Pain points
Fear of immigration enforcement, anxiety about surveillance, and the cost of the “experience economy.”

The Domestic “Main Eventer”

Motivations
Travel from neighboring states, like 75% of fans, to be part of a global spectacle.
Pain points
High accommodation costs, airport delays, and frustration with broken amenities like chargers and gates.

04 — Ideation

From sketches to a flow

Process

With the format already set, ideation focused on how WayATL should look, feel and behave, so we used AI-assisted development to explore that space quickly. Working across several AI generators, we produced a wide range of wireframe iterations, each testing a different take on layout, navigation and how a fan would actually move through the experience.

Generating many directions in parallel let us compare options side by side and spot what worked across versions. From there, we narrowed the strongest ideas into a single direction to carry into prototyping. AI widened the field; design judgment decided what stayed.

Features & structure

From features to structure

Once the iterations surfaced what fans needed most, we grouped every feature into clear themes, then turned those themes into the information architecture behind WayATL’s navigation.

Activated by QR codes / NFC chips

Every visit starts with a scan

WayATL is a mobile-first web experience activated by physical towers. To reach it, a fan scans one of these towers, whether it’s FIFA’s official signage or a tabletop sign.

06 — Testing

What testing told us

Method

We tested WayATL over multiple rounds using two complementary methods. Heuristic evaluations with experienced UI/UX professionals surfaced usability issues early. Moderated guerrilla testing with fans unfamiliar with Atlanta showed how real visitors navigate under pressure and gave us task-level usability metrics.

84%task completion rate
8.5/10average ease-of-use rating
7 secsmedian time to successfully complete task

Qualitative insight

What fans told us

Beyond the metrics, two themes came up again and again: WayATL needed to look like the World Cup, and its core actions needed to be impossible to miss.

0112 of 12 participants

The branding didn’t feel like the World Cup

We had built the UI on Metro Atlanta Chamber’s brand identity, since the Chamber sits on Atlanta’s FIFA Host Committee. Fans expected the product to look like the event itself.

“This product is for FIFA, but it doesn’t look like it.”UX professional

What we changedWe rebuilt the visual identity around FIFA’s World Cup 2026 branding.

027 of 12 participants

The core flows didn’t stand out

The Transit, Explore and SOS buttons were too small and plain to signal that they were the app’s main actions.

“I can’t tell that these buttons are the most important.”FIFA fan

What we changedWe enlarged and highlighted the three core buttons so they take up most of the home screen.

07 — Solution

One PWA for match day

Finding WayATL before they need it

QR codes and NFC chips appear on towers, hotel tabletops, restaurant tables, and airport arrivals, reaching people at quieter moments, long before they are standing in a crowd.

Mobile-first PWA

WayATL is a mobile-first website built to Progressive Web App (PWA) standards: accessible on any smartphone, save-able to a home screen, with native location support. No app store. No friction for international visitors.

Auto-language detection

WayATL detects the user’s native phone settings from the first tap and customizes the language experience accordingly.

Prototype walkthrough

Every screen, start to finish

From the first scan to a call for help, here is each WayATL screen in motion.

01

Splash screen

The entry point, activated the moment a fan taps a tower or scans a QR code. No account required. The experience is live in under a second, in the fan’s native language.

02

Homepage

Auto-detects the tower’s location and places the fan on the map, with no input required. Three primary actions surface immediately: Transit, Explore and SOS. FIFA 2026 destinations are preloaded and accessible without navigating or searching.

03

Transit

Every transport option, from walking and MARTA to micro-mobility and rideshare, is shown from the fan’s current position. Landmark-based directions give human-readable cues alongside the map. A step-free routing toggle prioritizes accessible pathways for visitors who need them.

04

Explore

Shows popular attractions nearby for fans to explore, and surfaces essentials such as food, restrooms, hydration points and first aid based on the fan’s exact location. Each result includes the walking distance and a direct link to navigate in the fan’s native map app.

05

SOS

911 and the FIFA 2026 emergency hotline are preloaded and one tap away. The nearest first aid location surfaces automatically based on the tower’s location. When FIFA command declares an emergency, a live evacuation map activates and its paths update in real time.

Multilingual screens

Speaks the fan’s language

The same homepage, served in each fan’s phone language from the first tap, including a full right-to-left layout for Arabic.

08 — Reflection

What I’d carry forward

Designing for a crowd taught me to design for a fan’s most stressful moment, not their average one.

Starting from the physical world shaped every decision. Because fans reach WayATL by scanning a tower, there is no download, no account and no language to choose, and the homepage already knows where they are standing. Testing kept us honest: every participant told us the first design didn’t feel like the World Cup, and moving to FIFA’s identity tied the product to the event fans came for.

With more time, I would test on-site during a live match day, where crowds, noise and weak signal are real rather than simulated, and validate the SOS flow and live evacuation map with the people who would run them.

Leading a two-person team over ten weeks, with AI-assisted tools speeding us from sketches to wireframes, showed me where my time matters most: less on polish, more on the questions only real users can answer.
