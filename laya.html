LAYA

Private equity leadership intelligence

Laya is a private equity leadership intelligence platform that helps newly PE-backed executives get governance-ready in their first 100 days. When a PE firm buys a company, it runs exhaustive diligence on financials, markets and legal risk. Then it hands the CEO a deal book and expects them to perform at PE speed. Laya closes that gap with an advisory dashboard, a live governance risk map, a PE-specific learning path, a private workspace, and Counsel, an AI advisor that knows the deal.

01 — Problem

A hundred days, no map

Context

CEOs owned by Private Equity (PE) firms must master everything a great company CEO does while operating at a higher metabolic rate. A newcomer to PE also faces having limited access to insight about the road ahead, because there is so little specific guidance in print about the portfolio-company CEO role.

75%

of PE failures tied to CEO culture fit

90%

of PE firms use a 100-day plan

73%

of underperforming CEOs are replaced

Problem statement

Private equity firms conduct exhaustive due diligence on financials, markets, and legal risk, yet consistently fail to evaluate or prepare for the single greatest post-investment risk: whether the CEO can lead effectively under PE governance. The gap between what PE firms analyze before close and what they support after close is where value creation dies.

How might we

…design a leadership transition system that protects PE value creation by closing the governance gap between when a deal closes and when the CEO is truly equipped to execute?

Case studies

Two real-world PE transitions, one that failed and one that succeeded, show how much the outcome depends on what happens to leadership after the deal closes.

Case study 01

Toys “R” Us

In 2005, Bain Capital, KKR and Vornado Realty Trust took Toys “R” Us private in a $6.6 billion leveraged buyout, just as Walmart, Target and Amazon were reshaping toy retail.

  • Financial doomThe deal loaded the company with more than $5 billion in debt from day one. Interest alone consumed hundreds of millions a year that should have funded stores, e-commerce and lower prices.
  • Debt obligationsStore reinvestment budgets were repeatedly cut to service the debt. Former executives later testified the company had the revenue to compete, but never the capital.
  • Paid either wayThe owners kept collecting millions in management and transaction fees, even as stores deteriorated.
Case study 02

Dell Technologies

In 2013, Michael Dell and Silver Lake took Dell private for $24.9 billion: Dell invested $4.2 billion for a 75% stake, Silver Lake $1.4 billion for 25%.

  • Complete visibilitySilver Lake’s operating partners supported management rather than replacing it, adding expertise in scaling, new markets and governance that complemented the founder’s vision.
  • An active, understood boardSilver Lake co-CEO Egon Durban joined the board, and the CEO knew exactly what that meant. Together they steered acquisitions including the roughly $67 billion EMC deal in 2016.
  • Massive ROIBy 2023, Silver Lake and Michael Dell had earned an estimated $70 billion, one of the most successful private equity turnarounds of all time.

Where momentum quietly breaks

01

Silent struggles

Despite the capital, the ambition and the accomplishments, newly PE-backed companies often find themselves off balance. Leadership is in disarray over expectations and miscommunication, and nobody knows who’s actually driving what.

02

Hidden sources of friction

Misaligned expectations, governance fog, priority whiplash, culture shock and timeline confusion are invisible culprits that sabotage post-close momentum.

03

The sponsor’s confidence spiral

The sponsor grows frustrated by a lack of urgency, missing strategic traction, unclear feedback and misunderstood reporting expectations. Add vague communication, PE culture shock and a poorly defined operating cadence, and a once high-performing management team starts to stall.

Insight 01

It’s rarely competence. It’s context.

Exceptional leaders struggle because they’re decoding an unfamiliar governance model while running a company. Constant monitoring leaves no bandwidth for the strategy that justified the deal.

Insight 02

CEOs execute a plan they didn’t write.

The VCP is built before or at close, often without the CEO. As mentor Raman Narasimhan (Mekiki Partners) put it, this creates a structural gap.

Insight 03

The problem is growing, not stabilizing.

PE-backed companies discuss CEO succession at about half the rate of public companies, most succession is reactive, and a survey by AlixPartners shows misalignment worsening after 10 years.

Elias Thorne

Founder-CEO · Thorne Aerospace · Backed by Meridian Capital

“

I know this business better than anyone alive. But I don’t know who I’m working for anymore, or what language they’re speaking.

Elias founded Thorne Aerospace 18 years ago and built it to $85M in enterprise value before accepting a buyout from Meridian Capital Partners, a mid-market PE firm. He remains as CEO under a 3-year earnout tied to EBITDA targets. He is still in the same office, with the same team, but everything about how he is governed has changed.

Values and goals
Preserve what he built

Elias spent 18 years building Thorne Aerospace. His deepest fear is watching PE operational pressure hollow it out. Protecting his people is a moral commitment, not a management priority.

Earn credibility at the board level

He wants to be seen as a strategic executive, not just the founder of a company that was acquired. Proving his value to Meridian Capital in the first 100 days is both a business requirement and a matter of identity.

Protect his earnout

A three-year EBITDA-linked earnout represents both financial security and validation. Missing targets doesn’t just cost him money, it signals failure.

Master the new operating model

Elias is driven by competence. Not knowing the rules of his own game is intolerable. His goal is to understand PE governance, financial language and value creation logic well enough to operate with the same confidence he had as a founder.

Build trust before influence

He knows he cannot lead effectively in this new environment until the right people genuinely trust him.

Frustrations
Governance fog

No clear map of decision rights. The ambiguity forces him to either ask basic questions that undermine his credibility, or guess and risk overstepping.

Language barrier with the sponsor

Terms like MOIC, EBITDA bridge, hold period thesis and working capital normalization are used in board meetings as if he already knows them. He nods along and decodes later.

Priority whiplash

What feels like a sensible 12-month initiative to him is treated as an urgent priority by the board. What he considers a crisis is sometimes met with calm indifference.

No onboarding was provided

The deal closed. Meridian celebrated. Then everyone went back to their day jobs and expected him to perform at PE speed immediately.

Identity displacement

He was the final authority for 18 years. Now he is one level in a hierarchy that has multiple layers above him, and those layers have opinions about his business.

Behaviors
Cross-references everything

Runs board communications through both digital tools and handwritten notes. Processes analytically and intuitively at the same time.

Listens before speaking in new rooms

In Meridian meetings, he has learned to observe tone, language and priority signals before committing to a position.

Maps power before acting

Identifies informal influence and engages it first.

Protects his senior team from PE friction

Deliberately absorbs board pressure before it reaches his direct reports, shielding the team from governance turbulence so they can keep executing.

Over-prepares for board calls

Arrives with pre-built narratives, variance explanations and contingency answers.

Private note-taker

Keeps a parallel private record of observations, decisions and political dynamics.

Attitudes and beliefs
Formal documentation is a formality

He believes the real operating system of any organization is its unwritten rules: who actually makes decisions, who has informal veto power, what gets approved without a meeting. He will always seek the real map, not the org chart.

Speed is the new currency

He has spent his career building deliberately. PE has compressed his decision horizon from years to quarters. He is uncomfortable with this but intellectually accepts it and is trying to rewire his instincts.

He earned the right to have opinions

Elias built an $85M company from nothing. He doesn’t take well to being managed or second-guessed on operational matters. He respects expertise but bristles at process for its own sake.

PE boards are not enemies, but not partners yet

He is pragmatic enough to know Meridian’s involvement can be valuable. But trust must be earned both ways. He is watching how the board behaves before deciding how open to be.

Asking for help is a sign of readiness, not weakness

In the founder world, self-sufficiency was survival. In the PE world, he is learning that seeking orientation is what sophisticated executives do.

Jia Lee

Installed CEO · Hartwell Medical Devices · Backed by Crestfield Equity Partners

“

I know exactly what the board wants. What I don’t know is whether anyone in this building will help me get there, or quietly wait for me to fail.

Jia is a three-time PE-placed CEO who knows the sponsor’s language fluently. What she doesn’t know is the company she’s been installed to lead. Brought in by Crestfield Equity Partners with a mandate to nearly double EBITDA in 36 months, she is managing a board she can read perfectly and a team that didn’t choose her and may not trust her yet.

Values and goals
Deliver the value creation plan, and fast

Jia was hired with a specific mandate: grow EBITDA from $12M to $22M in 36 months and position Hartwell for a strategic exit. She has done this before. She is here to execute, not explore.

Build trust before deploying authority

She has been parachuted in over people who have worked here for a decade. She knows that exercising authority before earning trust will create resistance.

Identify and retain the hidden performers

Every company has three or four people who quietly carry disproportionate institutional knowledge. Finding them in the first 30 days, before they test the market, is her highest-priority intelligence operation.

Separate the personal from the structural

Some resistance to her appointment is cultural and will dissolve with time. Some is structural and must be addressed decisively. She values being able to tell the two apart quickly.

Protect her reputation with the sponsor

Crestfield placed her. If Hartwell underperforms, it reflects on her track record in PE circles, a small world. Maintaining sponsor confidence is both a career imperative and a personal standard.

Frustrations
No company context at onboarding

Crestfield provided a deal book and a financial model. Nobody gave her a cultural map, a relationship guide or a briefing. She is spending her most critical relationship-building weeks gathering basic intelligence.

The board wants results before she has context

The tension between board timeline expectations and the operational reality of learning a new company is a source of constant friction.

Her PE experience reads as cold to a founder-culture team

She speaks in KPIs, frameworks and exit multiples because that is the language she was trained in. The team reads this as indifference to what they care about. She knows it’s happening and doesn’t know how to bridge it.

Every PE company is different

She has done three of these, and they are never the same. She wishes there were a smarter way to build a company-specific picture faster, so she can apply her experience sooner.

Behaviors
Runs leadership meetings immediately

Books 1:1s with every direct report and key informal leader in week one. Asks more than she answers. Records everything.

Separates board management from team management

Maintains two distinct communication modes: board-ready precision with Crestfield, conversational openness with the internal team.

Builds a shadow org map

Charts informal power structures in parallel with the official org chart.

Hunts quick wins in week two

Identifies two or three visible, near-term wins that signal competence to both the board and the team without disrupting what’s working.

Holds her VCP close until week four

Refuses to share her value creation plan draft until she has enough company context to make it credible, and resists board pressure to produce it earlier.

Attitudes and beliefs
Culture is data, not sentiment

She reads culture analytically. Meeting energy, response times, what gets volunteered vs. withheld: these are signals about where resistance, momentum and loyalty live.

The first 30 days are observational, not executive

She believes CEOs who arrive and immediately restructure destroy institutional knowledge. She spends the first month listening, mapping and building a picture before making structural moves.

Grief is a legitimate organizational response

Teams mourn leaders, and the former CEO may have been beloved. She doesn’t compete with that legacy. She acknowledges it and builds alongside it.

PE experience is both an asset and a liability

Her fluency in PE governance makes her effective with the board, but it can make her seem cold to a team that valued the founder’s warmth. She actively manages that perception gap.

Speed and thoroughness are not opposites

Moving fast on wrong information costs more than taking two extra days to get the picture right. She is fast because she is disciplined, not because she is reckless.

Kavitha Rajan

First-time CFO · Vantis Health Technologies · Backed by Harlow Growth Partners

“

I have spent 12 years becoming exceptional at finance. Nobody told me that a PE acquisition would require me to become a different kind of executive overnight.

Kavitha joined Vantis Health Technologies four years ago as Director of Finance and rapidly became the operational backbone of the company’s financial function. When Harlow Growth Partners completed their acquisition of Vantis, the outgoing CFO took a role at another firm, and Kavitha was promoted into the seat with 48 hours’ notice.

Values and goals
Prove the promotion was deserved, not circumstantial

She knows the timing of her elevation had as much to do with the CFO’s departure as with her own readiness. She is determined to make that irrelevant and perform beyond the expected level.

Build a finance function fit for PE oversight

She inherited a competent but founder-era finance team built for quarterly reviews, not monthly PE reporting packages. Rebuilding its cadence, tooling and output without losing the people she depends on is her most urgent structural goal.

Become a trusted voice in the boardroom

She has spent her career in the room next to the boardroom. She wants the kind of credibility that makes a board trust a CFO’s numbers without demanding a recut.

Protect her team through the acceleration

3 of her 4 senior finance staff have never worked under PE ownership. She wants to get them through the transition without burnout, departures or errors that reach the board.

Own the CFO role, not just perform it

Her long-term goal is to develop the strategic instincts, governance fluency and executive presence that make her a genuine peer to the CEO and the board, not just a technically excellent subordinate.

Frustrations
Promoted with 48 hours’ notice and zero handover

The outgoing CFO left within a week of the acquisition close. There was no onboarding. She inherited a role, a team and a set of live deliverables with no context.

Expected to know PE reporting formats she has never seen

Every format is new, and she is expected to produce them at board standard from day one.

Her background adds friction to prove

She is the only person in the boardroom who relocated internationally and whose first professional training was in a non-US system. She feels the extra weight of demonstrating competence at a higher resolution.

Her team is running at a cadence it wasn’t built for

Founder-era processes are straining under monthly PE reporting.

She can’t tell board confidence from board tolerance

She can’t tell whether the board genuinely trusts her work or is patiently waiting for her to grow into the role.

Behaviors
Arrives first, stays latest

Before every board pack deadline she runs her numbers twice, asks what question each number will generate and prepares.

Documents every new process in real time

Every PE reporting format she meets for the first time is immediately documented as a replicable process.

Seeks patterns across board interactions

After every board touchpoint she writes a private debrief.

Builds financial narratives, not just models

A flawless model without a clear narrative generates more board anxiety than a simpler model that tells a coherent story.

Protects her team from unfiltered board pressure

She absorbs board pressure and translates it into clear tasks for her team.

Uses her international background as an asset

She reads the boardroom with the attentiveness she developed crossing professional cultures, and adapts accordingly.

Attitudes and beliefs
Competence is the only currency that transfers

Her professional identity is built on the quality of her work. If the output is exceptional, credibility will follow, and nothing else substitutes for it.

PE is a system, and systems can be learned

She isn’t intimidated by PE governance, just frustrated by the lack of a structured way to learn it. She treats the transition as an engineering problem. She just needs the manual.

Transparency with the CEO builds everything

She refuses to manage up by filtering bad news. A CEO deciding on accurate information always beats the illusion of stability.

Cultural fluency is a professional skill

Having relocated from Bengaluru to Austin, she code-switches between professional cultures with precision, and treats PE governance as a new language to master, not a barrier.

It’s the cognitive cost, not competence

The failure of PE-backed executives is rarely about competence. It’s about the cognitive and psychological cost of navigating an unfamiliar governance model while running a company. Three research-backed phenomena explain why even exceptional leaders struggle.

Role incongruence

A leader’s self-concept clashes with new role expectations. The “neutral zone,” when the old identity has been shed but the new one hasn’t formed, is where most PE-backed CEOs spend their first 100 days.

EBM Software · The Hidden Weight of the First 100 Days, 2026
Cognitive overload

Decoding PE terminology, producing unfamiliar financial reports, managing the board and leading a disoriented team at once leaves no bandwidth for the strategy that justified the deal. Constant monitoring creates what researchers call “cognitive reservation.”

Imposter syndrome at elevation

A newly elevated leader may struggle when their self-concept clashes with new role expectations. Often the gap is context, not competence: meeting PE-specific formats for the first time, at full professional accountability.

The mandate every CEO inherits on day 1

Value creation plan (VCP) · Bain & Company Global Private Equity Report 2026

The structured blueprint PE firms use to grow a portfolio company’s enterprise value over the hold period, combining financial targets, operational initiatives, talent strategy and governance around the investment thesis. The CEO is expected to execute it, even if they didn’t help build it. Today’s deals need 10–12% average annual EBITDA growth to hit a 2.5× MOIC target, double the 5% that was enough in the prior decade.

EBITDAEarnings before interest, taxes, depreciation & amortization

The primary measure of company value in PE. Purchase price, exit value, earnout and KPIs are all expressed as a multiple of EBITDA. Growing it is the CEO’s central mandate.

Value = EBITDA × exit multiple
MOICMultiple on invested capital

How many times the PE firm has multiplied its original investment. A 2.5× MOIC means the firm returned two and a half times its capital. Most buyout funds target 2.5× or higher.

MOIC = exit value ÷ capital
IRRInternal rate of return

The annualized return on investment. Top-quartile PE funds target 20–25% IRR. Highly sensitive to timing: a 3× MOIC over 4 years gives ~26% IRR; over 7 years, just ~17%.

3× in 4 yrs ≈ 26% IRR
EarnoutPerformance-linked deferred payment

A portion of the acquisition price paid to the founder only if the company hits post-close financial targets. It’s how PE firms align the founder’s incentives with the investment thesis.

Design system

The visual language behind Laya, from principles and color to the components it’s built from.

Light from darkness

The background is near-black. Orange light radiates from interactive elements only. Every glow is earned by context and function, never decoration.

Warmth over precision

Laya is not a cold institutional tool. Kugile sets the display text, surfaces are brown-black rather than navy, and the platform feels like a trusted presence.

Executive density

Information-rich but never overwhelming. No gamification, no badges, no streaks. Whitespace respects the user’s cognitive load at the highest-pressure moments of their career.

Glow as hierarchy

Level 0 (resting) to Level 4 (hero). Glow shows where the user is in the interaction space. It is never applied to text, and always stays in the orange family.

06 — Solution

A briefing, not a dashboard

A dashboard that opens with a directive

Each morning starts with a greeting and one line of real-time context: how many priorities, which decision is pending, what Counsel flagged overnight. Today’s Priorities shows only what is actionable that day, set against the 100-day clock.

Counsel, an advisor that knows your deal

Counsel is calibrated to the leader’s own board, covenants, earnout and 100-day window, so its answers come in context, not as generic advice. Shared deal documents stay private; the PE firm never sees them.

Governance risk map

A single governance score, banded red, amber or green, breaks down into the risk areas behind it. Counsel explains what is moving the score, and each risk links straight to the module or tool that addresses it.

Prototype walkthrough

Dashboard

07 — Reflection

What I’d carry forward

The discomfort was intentional. The growth was the goal.

Laya was a deliberate choice. Private equity is complex, high-stakes and completely outside my prior experience, and that was the point. I wanted to push into territory I had never designed for, where the domain demanded mastery before the design could begin. My SCADpro work with Deloitte and UBS gave me a foundation in enterprise environments and financial services culture, but Laya asked me to go further.

A capstone also gave me free rein to experiment, and I used it. I didn’t want Laya to look like the typical corporate software these leaders already use every day, so I gave it an immersive, cinematic feel: a product that earns attention, not one that blends into the stack.
